AWS's own What's New post, dated November 10, announces "the general availability of the Web Search API on Amazon Bedrock AgentCore". AgentCore is AWS's service for running AI agents. Queries "are billed at $7 per 1,000 queries, the same rate as Web Search through AgentCore Gateway, with no gateway charges."

The post names six regions. In the US, there's North Virginia, Ohio and Oregon. In Asia Pacific, there's Sydney and Tokyo. In Europe, there's Ireland.

This is AWS's own launch post. I have no outside test of the service stored.

My read is this, at moderate conviction. Web search for agents is turning into a per-query line item. The unit of cost is the query, not the seat. At $7 per thousand, one query costs seven tenths of a cent. That sounds like nothing until you count. An agent that checks supplier news, freight conditions or commodity prices runs searches in loops. It does it on a schedule, for every part number or every lane. Before you wire an agent to live web data, estimate how many queries it will make in a day and in a month. Then put that number in the budget next to the token cost. It's the same discipline as with tokens. Count the units before you turn it on.

Check the region list against where your data is allowed to live, too. If your shop has rules about that, six regions is a short list.

Google DeepMind's own post, dated November 10, introduces AlphaProof Code. DeepMind calls it "a multi-LLM agentic framework". The post says researchers guided AI agents "to mathematically prove key security properties of the actual code underlying the Signal protocol". Agents powered by Gemini Carbon did the proofs, "generating over six million lines of Lean". Lean is a language for writing machine-checked proofs.

Then the agents audited their own proof. According to the post, they "highlighted areas that needed a second look by a human – just one percent of the codebase". The verification took, in DeepMind's words, "just four weeks".

To test the method, the team "reintroduced two real, previously fixed bugs into an isolated version of Signal's code. In both cases, the system produced a machine-checked proof that the buggy version was insecure." The post quotes Signal's CTO, Ehren Kret. DeepMind says it's "open-sourcing our formal verification artifacts".

All of that is DeepMind's account, plus a quote from Signal. Nothing outside the post confirms it.

My read is this, and it's speculative. What might carry over to your floor is the split of labor. The agents did the bulk of the work, then checked it, and sent a small slice to people. A back office could copy that pattern for exception review. Let the system work the full set of invoices or orders, and have it flag the slice a person should look at. The post doesn't claim this applies to anything like that. That's my extrapolation.

Medium term, meaning next year, the cost of an AI workflow becomes a sum of units. Tokens in. Tokens out. Cached tokens. Queries. Maybe more meters to come. The shops that do well will be the ones that can say what one order, one invoice or one exception costs to process. A monthly total won't be enough.

Long term, and this is speculative, I expect some of your vendors to be public companies with public numbers. That gives you something you rarely get from a supplier: their margins, in writing, on a schedule. It's leverage at contract time, if someone on your side reads it.

The position to take now is simple. Price every AI workflow by the unit, at the rate that will still be in force after the introductory period. Then cap it.
