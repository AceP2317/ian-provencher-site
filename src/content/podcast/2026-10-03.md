Second: Mercor, and what "almost flawless" measures.

Sourcing, carefully. Mercor published a study this week. Its own post sits on mercor.com, which I cannot open from here. So the study's figures are relayed: I read The Decoder's write-up, dated the second of October, directly. Separately, I read the benchmark paper itself, on arXiv, version two, dated the thirtieth of July. That paper is the earlier, harder benchmark that the study draws on. It is first-hand but it is from July, not from this week.

What the study reports, as The Decoder relays it: twelve licensed CPA accountants, averaging five and a half years of experience, worked through simplified tasks from Mercor's accounting benchmark. Quote: "Eighteen months ago, the best models still scored below the accountants' average of about 37 percent. Today, they solve the same tasks almost flawlessly." The Decoder's summary line: "AI models are faster, more accurate, and far cheaper than accountants at structured bookkeeping tasks."

Then the same write-up turns. The full benchmark has a hundred sixty tasks across ten simulated companies. The Decoder says Claude Opus 5.5 currently leads with sixty-one point eight percent of grading criteria met, Fable 5.1 follows at sixty-one point zero, and GPT-6 Astra at fifty-seven point nine. And, quote: "Mercor says no model fully solved almost 60 percent of the tasks." The Decoder's own line: "AI models can't close the books without oversight yet." And it relays Mercor's admission that the study's tasks "test exactly what AI does best, which is hunting down details and following instructions precisely," while leaving out client conversations, check-ins with colleagues, and context built up over years. The Decoder adds Mercor's conclusion: "Mercor says that's why accountants can't be replaced, though it expects major productivity gains across the industry."

So here are three results from one company about one profession: tasks solved almost flawlessly, about sixty-two percent of rubric criteria met, and nearly sixty percent of tasks that no model fully solved. They are not in conflict. They are three different questions, and I want to take them apart using the paper I did read.

The paper's primary score is called Mean Criteria. It is the share of a grading rubric's items that a model's answer meets, averaged over runs. Partial credit. A model that gets the reconciliation mostly right and misses the accrual scores well. The paper also reports two harder measures. The first, which it calls Pass at eight, asks whether a model passes a task at least once in eight tries. The second, which it calls Pass to the eighth, asks whether it produces a fully correct output in all eight tries.

The results, from the July paper: the best at-least-once score is twenty-one point five percent. The best every-time score is two point six percent. The paper's words: "Consistency is low across the board," and the numbers are, quote, "demonstrating how far agents are from reliably completing accounting work end to end."

Two caveats on that. The first is mine, from the dates: these are July numbers on July's models, and the leaderboard has moved since; The Decoder's figures are newer, and I cannot tell you what the every-time score is today. The second is from the paper's limitations section: the benchmark was built hard on purpose, and tasks were filtered to those where three frontier models scored low. So two point six is not a measure of typical close work. It is a measure of how consistent the models are on work chosen to be difficult.

What the benchmark covers, and leaves out. Each task world, quote, "is a self-contained company frozen at month-end close." Four task types: reconciliation, data entry, variance analysis, and schedules and accruals. Reconciliation is the biggest, sixty-one of the hundred sixty tasks. Schedules and accruals is the hardest category, which the paper puts down to its "multi-step, judgment-heavy nature." It leaves out tax, audit, consolidation, multi-entity and multi-currency work, and external reporting. And the authors excluded any task that needs a human in the loop. In their words: "This removes a skill that real staff accountants exercise constantly; knowing when to ask a clarifying question rather than proceed on an assumption."

The operator's read, flagged as mine. You know the manufacturing word for this: repeatability. A process that is right once is not a process that is right every time, and the every-time score asks for eight consecutive good lots. First-pass yield is the neighboring idea: the share of units that come off the line right, with no rework. A month-end close is not graded on whether one run, once, produced the right figure. It is graded on whether the posting is right every period without someone re-checking it. In SAP terms, the category the paper finds hardest, schedules and accruals, looks like an accrual estimate that needs someone to know why the number changed. That mapping is mine; the paper does not use SAP terms.

What I can say is narrower: part of the dispute is about one setting. Meta says reading Messages requires Full Disk Access, and the columnist says it was off. By Apple's own description that setting largely sidesteps the Mac's other privacy controls, and the company that makes the operating system has just said it will add controls around it, and that the risk grows as agents become more capable.

The operator's read, flagged as mine. Full Disk Access is the permission people grant to make an app work when it will not work otherwise. An owner sets up an AI assistant on a Mac, it stalls on a permission prompt, and the fix people reach for is to turn on Full Disk Access. One sentence in Apple's post is the one for you: for communication apps, it "can also compromise the privacy of the people users are communicating with." On a small manufacturer's machine, those people are suppliers, customers and the bank. Their messages are the data.

You can check this in about five minutes. On any Mac that you or a client uses, open System Settings, Privacy and Security, Full Disk Access, and read the list. That path is from my own knowledge of macOS, not from Apple's post. Every entry on it should be something you can name and justify.

Fourth: what a model's hidden reasoning leaks, and who can read it.

Sourcing. OpenAI published a post this week about stopping what it calls an adversarial distillation campaign. OpenAI's site returned a bot-challenge page to me, even through a real browser, so I did not read that post. I read The Decoder's report of it, dated the first of October. I also read, directly, the research paper that The Decoder says OpenAI credits: "Stealing Reasoning Traces from Proprietary LLM APIs," on arXiv, submitted the tenth of August. The researchers also published an update, on a site I cannot open; The Decoder's account of it is relayed.

The OpenAI side, as The Decoder reports it: activity began on July first, spiked to sixteen thousand requests from more than four thousand users on July twenty-fourth and twenty-fifth, and OpenAI says it had shut down a network of more than fifteen thousand accounts with related patterns by July twenty-eighth. The Decoder writes that a footnote clarifies these were attempted extractions, "not necessarily successful ones." OpenAI links a core group to people associated with Moonshot AI, and says it is unclear whether all the actors trace to a single source. That is an allegation by OpenAI, relayed. The Decoder's report carries no response from Moonshot. A search summary I read says Moonshot did not respond to CNBC's request for comment; I could not open CNBC.

The method matters more to you than the attribution. Models that reason first hand the client their reasoning only as an encrypted block, and the client passes it back with the next request. The paper's finding is that those blocks are interchangeable across sessions, users and models within one provider. So a weaker, cheaper model from the same provider can be handed a stronger model's encrypted block and made to write it out in plain text.

Now the part from the paper I read, which is about you and not about Moonshot. The authors collected six thousand seven hundred eight public agent session logs from GitHub and Hugging Face, produced by Claude, GPT and Gemini models, and decoded the reasoning blocks still sitting in them: three hundred fifteen thousand three hundred twenty. They report that point three percent of the decoded blocks contained at least one privacy leak, and that four point nine percent of the sessions leaked at least one real sensitive item. From genuine, non-benchmark user sessions, the recovered items include, quote, "62 distinct API keys, 33 passwords, 24 access tokens, 7 private keys, 30 personal emails." Those are counts from a scan the authors themselves call non-exhaustive, and they come from a large language model judge; the paper says so.

Here is the line I would underline. Quote: "when the user asks the agent to anonymize or 'clean up' the session, the model re-reads the full history in its hidden reasoning and restates the sensitive values there that need to be removed." You scrub the visible text, you publish the log, and the secret is still in a block you cannot read, which someone else can.

Their advice, which is plain: strip "all reasoning blocks and opaque reasoning fields from transcripts prior to public release" where any secret or private information was exposed to the agent, and do not retain or commit "raw API transcripts containing signatures in shared repositories," even if the plaintext has been sanitized.

Two limits on all of this, from the paper's own text. The testing was, in its words, "as of our testing period (early July 2026)." And after disclosure, quote, "All model providers acknowledged the receipt of our report and subsequently we were unable to launch the same attacks." The paper says only that the authors could no longer launch the same attacks. It does not say which endpoints. The phrase "own APIs" in what follows comes from The Decoder's September account, not from the paper.

The Decoder's account of the researchers' update is the new part, and it is about platforms, not models. Per The Decoder, when the researchers tested again on September thirteenth, the attack was blocked on OpenAI's and Anthropic's own APIs, but on Microsoft Azure it worked against every OpenAI model they tried, including GPT-6 Astra, and against Anthropic models up to Sonnet 5. One of the researchers is quoted: "Same models, but different protections depending on which platform serves them." Per the researchers' timeline, as The Decoder relays it, OpenAI added safeguards to the Azure endpoint on September twenty-seventh, and the Anthropic extraction could no longer be reproduced there from September twenty-eighth. So by their account this was a gap that stayed open for weeks and had safeguards added before OpenAI's post, not one I can tell you is open today. The Decoder adds that the researchers describe the fixes so far as piecemeal and superficial, and that OpenAI, in the Decoder's words, "acknowledges that models hosted by partners need the same protection as its own services and says the work is not finished." The Decoder also reports a second, simpler method, in which the model is given a notepad tool and told to write its reasoning there. Per The Decoder it worked on every OpenAI model and on Opus 4.8 and Sonnet 5, and not on Opus 5, Fable 5 or Fable 5.1; it gives no fix date for that one. I have the Decoder's account of the update, not the update.

Operator's read, flagged as mine. Two practical lines. One: if you or a client's developer keeps agent transcripts, treat the raw transcript as a credential-bearing file. Strip the reasoning fields before it goes in a repo, a shared drive or a support ticket, and do not trust a log just because the agent was asked to clean it up. Two: when you quote a client a model, you are also choosing a front door. The same model served from a different cloud can have different protections, and a vendor deck will not tell you which. Ask where the model is served from and who patches that path.

Before we close, the AppliedIQ angle.

Put the day side by side. Anthropic says the scarce thing is the person who can scope a deployment, test it and hand it over. Mercor's numbers say a model can look near perfect on a bounded, simplified task, while on its harder benchmark, as of July, no model produced a fully correct output on all eight tries for more than two point six percent of tasks. Apple says the permission that lets backup apps work can also expose everything on a machine, and agents raise the stakes. And the reasoning paper says the log you scrubbed may still hold the secret. My reading: the model run is not the product. The product is the control around the run. Who scoped it, what it can touch, how often it is right, and what is in the paper trail.

For your world, that is a quality plan. In a plant, the question is not whether the machine can make a good part once. It is what the capability study says, what the control plan is, and who signs the first-article inspection. The value of the person you hire, or the firm you hire, is the control plan.

One concrete action, and two quick ones.

The action: before any AppliedIQ agent goes live for a client, run a repeat test on one real task. Take a task the client actually does, say matching a supplier's invoice to a receipt, or building an accrual schedule. Run it eight times on the same inputs. Count the runs where every output is fully right. Yesterday I told you to rerun ten of a client's real assignments on two models and count finished tasks and retries. That compares models. This tests one model against itself. If it is eight out of eight, record that as the measured result for that task, with the date and the model. If it is fewer, the task gets a named human sign-off in the offer and the client sees why. That is my method, borrowed from the paper's measure. It is not Mercor's recommendation. Eight runs is a small sample: eight out of eight is evidence of a good rate, not proof of a perfect one, and you should make sure the runs are not served from a cache. It gives the client a number to put in front of their auditor.

Two quick ones. First, the Full Disk Access check: five minutes on each Mac you run or support, and a one-line rule in every offer: no agent gets that permission without a named owner and a reason. Second, a decision for you and not for me. Getting started in Anthropic's partner network is free, in Anthropic's words. The same post says tiered partners receive discounted rates on their first exam attempt, which suggests the exams are not free for everyone; I do not know the price. A credential also ties you to one vendor. Yesterday I suggested a financing-and-dependency line for your vendor checklist. A credential is a dependency too, so ask a question of the same kind before you climb: what does it cost to leave? Whether joining is worth it for a firm your size is a call I cannot make from here.

That is my suggestion, not a fact about your market.

Two things to watch. Whether Apple names a macOS version for the new controls. And whether anyone publishes a repeat-run score, the every-time score, for a real accounting or ERP task on a current model, because that number would tell you more than any accuracy figure on a vendor's slide.
