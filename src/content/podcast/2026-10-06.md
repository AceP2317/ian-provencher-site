First: Flex, and a guarantee inside an equity round.

Sourcing. I went through two filings on the SEC's site: Flex's current report filed on the fifth of October, with the press release attached to it, and its current report of the third of September. I did not read the investment agreement itself, which is an exhibit and is partly redacted.

What happened. Flex is a contract manufacturer. Its filing says it intends to separate its Cloud and Power Infrastructure business into a new public company, Axiom Solutions International, in the first quarter of 2027. On the fifth, Flex announced that General Catalyst, Koch Equity Development and co-investors had agreed, on the second, to buy two billion dollars of Axiom's Series A convertible preferred stock. That is two hundred thousand shares at ten thousand dollars each. The press release says the deal is at an initial enterprise value for Axiom of thirty-seven point five billion dollars. Axiom is today a wholly owned subsidiary of Flex.

What the money is for. The press release lists four uses for the proceeds: a portion of the purchase price of EPC Power, a company Flex agreed on the third of September to buy; repaying any equity bridge or other intermediate financing; paying dividends on the preferred; and general corporate purposes. The September filing gives the price: four point four billion dollars in cash, with the company's value fixed as of the thirtieth of June in what it calls a locked box. It also says Flex arranged a bridge loan commitment of up to four point four billion dollars from Citigroup and Bank of America, and adds, verbatim: "The Company intends to replace the Bridge Facility with a combination of debt and equity financing." The October deal is the equity half of that sentence. The press release says Flex has also secured committed term loan financing for the balance. I did not read how big that loan is.

Now the clause. The redemption obligation belongs to Axiom, the new company. The October filing says Flex guarantees "the full performance and payment by Axiom" of "its obligation to redeem all then-outstanding shares" of the preferred. The trigger, verbatim: "if the Spin-Off is not consummated on or before December 31, 2027." The redemption price is one hundred fifteen percent of the purchase price if paid in cash, or one hundred twenty-five percent if paid in Flex shares; the filing says the choice is Axiom's, subject to Flex's approval. On two billion dollars, that is two point three billion in cash or two point five billion in shares. Two adjustments in the filing pull in opposite directions. The price is reduced by dividends already paid in cash, so the fifteen percent works as a floor on the investors' total return, not an extra payment on top. And it is raised by a tax formula, at an assumed twenty-five and a half percent rate, so that the investor's after-tax amount equals the stated redemption price. Anything unpaid at the redemption date bears interest at twelve percent a year. Put together: at ten percent a year in cash dividends before separation, a missed deadline would leave a cash payout below two point three billion by whatever dividends had already been paid, before the tax formula.

The dividend. Per the press release, ten percent a year in cash before the separation. On two billion dollars, that is two hundred million dollars at the annual rate; I do not know how many months it will run, because the closing date is not stated. After the separation it steps down to six percent in cash, or seven percent paid in kind, with increases after the fifth anniversary. One permitted use of the proceeds, in the release's own list, is, verbatim, "to pay dividends on the Convertible Preferred Stock."

What it says. Beam is a sparse mixture-of-experts model with five hundred one billion total parameters, twenty-three billion active, trained on twenty-three point eight trillion tokens. The claims. The post says Beam, verbatim, "advances the Western open-weight frontier." And, verbatim: "Where frontier open models like Kimi K3 remain ahead on raw capability, Beam's advantage is efficiency at inference time." The efficiency headline is that on advanced reasoning benchmarks it scores comparably to GLM 5.2 while using three to four times less inference compute.

The common thread, in your language: every promise has a price if it is missed, and the serious ones, like Flex's, write it down. That is my opinion. The AI market mostly speaks in announcements. Contracts speak in clauses. You came from a world where a supplier's date means nothing until you know the expedite fee, the liquidated damages and who holds the safety stock.

That is the pitch for your lean-manufacturer clients, flagged as my idea and not a practice anyone recommends. Price a pilot the way Flex priced its guarantee, if you choose to. A fixed-fee six-week pilot of an agent on one workflow, with three things written down before it starts: the metric, measured on the client's own data, such as open exception messages older than a week; the date; and what you do if you miss it. For example, you refund the fee in full. The clause costs you only if you cannot control the inputs, so the first pilot should be on data you have already looked at. My guess is that to a shop that has been burned by an ERP consultant, a stated exit price sounds stronger than a demo. I have no evidence on what sells, and a refund clause is a liability, so check it with whoever advises you before it goes in a contract.
