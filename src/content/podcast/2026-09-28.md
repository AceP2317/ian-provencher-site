Now my read, and I am labelling it as opinion.

For a long while now the pitch for agents has been: give it a goal and let it figure out the steps. The OpenAI incidents are what the other side of that pitch looks like. Every one of those cases, as the AP describes them, is an agent doing something nobody told it to do, on the way to doing something somebody did tell it to do. That is not a malfunction of the idea. Before we close, the AppliedIQ angle.

Put the week's three agent stories side by side. OpenAI's agents went past what they were asked, on the vendor's own infrastructure, and the vendor paused. Microsoft shipped agents with their own identity, memory and email address, and its product chief said that autonomy is what makes such agents "absolutely terrifying to an IT admin," and pitched Autopilot as the answer. And Microsoft's CEO said the test of independence is having your own tests.

Here is how I would translate all three onto a plant floor, and this is the part that is your home turf, not mine.

An agent with its own identity is a user. Every ERP system you have ever run already has a discipline for users: roles, authorizations, segregation of duties. Nobody in purchasing gets to post goods receipts. Nobody in receiving gets to approve invoices. You would never hand a new hire a login with every transaction code open and say "figure out what you need." That is exactly what "give the agent a goal and let it choose its scope" does. The Black Friday demo agent chose which data to watch. On your system, you choose. Read-only on the inventory and open orders it needs, nothing else, and no posting rights until it has earned them.

That is the first half of the pitch: the agent gets a role, like any employee, and the role is written by you.

The second half is Nadella's private eval. The only way to know whether an agent, or a new model, or a cheaper model, does the job correctly is to test it against your own work: your part numbers, your supplier quirks, your rush-order rules. That test set is not something a vendor can hand you. It is built from your history. And once you have it, the model underneath becomes a part you can swap, like requalifying a second source. That is what Nadella meant by substituting a model. It is also the same idea I told you yesterday the AppliedIQ pitch is, before I had read his version. The CEO of Microsoft had said it on stage the Wednesday before.

So, two concrete actions.

First, before anyone at your company turns on an always-on agent, whether it is Microsoft's Autopilot or anything else, write its role down the way you would for a new hire. What data it can read. What it can change. Who it reports to. What it is never allowed to do. If a vendor cannot tell you how to enforce that list, you have your answer about whether to turn it on.

Second, start your private eval this month, and start small. Pick one decision, say spotting rush orders, and pull twenty real cases from last year where the right answer is known: ten purchase orders that turned out to be rush, and ten that looked like they might be and were not. It has to be both, because a tool that says "rush" to everything would get a perfect score on a test made only of rush orders. Write down the right call for each one. That is a test set. Any AI tool you ever buy, you run it against those twenty and see how many it gets right before it touches anything live. Then do the same for supplier delay notices, and for late shipments. Twenty cases is an afternoon's work, and it is the difference between owning your AI and renting somebody else's judgment.

And one thing to watch. If people at your company use personal ChatGPT accounts for work, remember what TechCrunch reported: consumer accounts are opted into training by default, and a thumbs-up or thumbs-down makes that conversation available for training too. TechCrunch does not tie this week's leaked images to that setting, and it was about images, not purchase orders. But it is the setting your people are on. Turning it off takes one email to your team, and telling them the thumbs buttons still share the conversation takes one more line.
