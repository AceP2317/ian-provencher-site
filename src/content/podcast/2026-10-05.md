First: the patch clock and the exploit clock.

What happened. Horizon3 is a security company. Its researcher Zach Hanley used Anthropic's Mythos model, which Horizon3 says it has had since joining Anthropic's Project Glasswing in July, to look for flaws in Rejetto HFS, an open-source web file server. The flaw is tracked as CVE-2026-61500. Horizon3's account: HFS makes the secret key that signs its login cookies with JavaScript's ordinary random-number function, which is not built to be secret, and the server also hands out outputs of that same generator to anyone who starts a login. Per the write-up's steps: sample that login endpoint twelve times, feed the numbers to a solver, recover the generator's state, work backwards to the signing key, forge an administrator cookie, and then use a built-in feature of the admin interface to run code on the server. No password needed, only the name of an account.

On what the model did, the write-up says it found the weak generator, found the separate place where the server leaks its output, and, verbatim, "recognized those two facts as a chain." It adds that Mythos then built a working proof of concept. And Horizon3 makes an economic claim, verbatim: "Vulnerabilities that take a significant amount of time to understand and develop exploits for are not prioritized for further weaponization." It gives two reasons Horizon3's own researchers had abandoned flaws like this one before, no deep mathematics background and no time or economic case, and says, verbatim: "Mythos negates both of those reasons." Read that with the vendor in mind. Horizon3 sells a security product, the page ends by offering a demonstration of it, and Horizon3 is a Glasswing partner. That is a company's account of a model it has an interest in. I cannot test the claim from here.

The Register's own subhead says "exploitation attempts." Nothing I read says anyone's server was taken over. Search summaries of other write-ups put the first exploitation on other days. I am using the one that quotes VulnCheck's own researcher.

Now the other clock. The project's release page shows version three point two point one was published on the thirteenth of July. Its notes, verbatim: "Multiple security vulnerabilities have been found in all previous versions, potentially allowing an attacker to gain administrative access to HFS." And it thanks Hanley of Horizon3, verbatim, "in collaboration with Claude and Anthropic Research." VulnCheck's tracker lists this CVE with a publication date of the thirteenth of July, the same day, and The Register tells readers to update to version three point two point one or later. The patch clock measures something else: how long a fix sat there while vulnerable servers stayed up. The release notes do not say how many servers that was.

Before we close, the AppliedIQ Angle.

Put the day side by side, as clocks. A flaw had a fix for seventy-nine days and was attacked about a day after its steps were published. A price list moved on the same day as a cheaper product. A data-center campus has a customer date and a regulator's date. A news item was dated today and was made in September. And the government reportedly gave its new task force a hundred and twenty days. The common thread, in your language: every claim about AI has a date it was true and a date it can change, and almost nobody writes down either.

You already run a business on this. A planning time fence says that inside this window the plan does not move. A quote has a validity date. A supplier's lead time is a number with an as-of date. The AI side of the market mostly speaks in nouns: a model, a box, a framework, a price. What I would bring to a client is the dates.

One concrete action, and two quick ones.

The action: build a one-page dependency ledger for the agent offer. One row for each thing the offer depends on: the model and its provider, the hardware tier, any open-source component you deploy, the rule or regulation it assumes. Four columns. The fact. The date you last confirmed it from a primary source. The clock it is on, meaning what could change it and roughly when. And who tells you. Fill it with today's rows: a twenty-four gigabyte card price with a date on it, the fixed version of every component you ship, and any retirement notice on a model you name. This is my method, built from today's stories. Nobody recommends it, and it predicts nothing. It only tells you which of your numbers have gone stale.

Quick one: patch lag. In every proposal write one line: which component, which version, the date of its newest security fix, and who applies updates and how fast. A thirty-day target for critical fixes is my suggestion, not a standard. Seventy-nine days is what you saw today. And if any client or demo of yours runs Rejetto HFS, make it version three point three point four or later this week. That is the release with the project's latest security fixes, and the fix for this flaw came earlier, in three point two point one. I do not know of any that does.

Quick two: when you quote hardware, put a validity date and a price-adjustment clause on the quote, the way you would on a commodity-indexed purchase order, and name the hardware by the memory it needs, not by a product name.

That is my suggestion, not a fact about your market.
