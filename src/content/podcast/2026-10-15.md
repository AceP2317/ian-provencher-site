The first correction is to yesterday's Anthropic quote, which I cut short. The full line reads: "Our experiments suggest that small AI models may be months away from being capable enough to self-replicate on commercial internet-of-things devices, like smart ovens and off-brand Android TV boxes." The clause I dropped, "on commercial internet-of-things devices," says what the target is. The dropped clause narrowed the claim, and my summary was too broad, so it is back in.

The second correction is the larger one, and it comes last in this section.

Start with the Anthropic post, published October twelfth. Its worm test ran on eight cloud nodes, each running an Android emulator. The GLM-5.2 worm spread to all eight nodes in four of five trials and seven of eight in the fifth. That is the replication result in Anthropic's simplified evaluation, and the post calls its own setup generous: the exploit step was kept trivial, because the debug port has no authentication. In the post's words, that "makes this an upper bound on real-world success." It also says there are "many other steps" to a real autonomous worm. A separate demonstration runs a twenty-six-billion-parameter Gemma model across fifteen off-brand Android TV boxes that cost about fifty-five dollars each, at 0.39 tokens per second. That is distributed inference, not a worm. The post keeps the two apart, and so will I.

The forecast is the sentence to hold. "If small models improve at their historical rate, they'll match this capability level by the end of this year." The post says that trend comes from a capability index, and it adds that the index "is only a proxy metric for replication ability." It also says that a replicator "would be challenging to shut down, since there's no centralized point to shut off." I found no outside group that has repeated the test. This is the lab's forecast about its own work, and it is the one I am treating as the claim.

The Justice Department made the second item public on October thirteenth. It unsealed a complaint against Abdulrahman Alfozan, thirty-five, a Saudi national who, according to the release, had been employed by OpenAI as an engineer since July 2025. He was arrested on October ninth. The release says OpenAI personnel discovered a recording device on or about September thirtieth, hidden in a USB port on the back of a television in an executive conference room in one of the company's San Francisco buildings. It describes a second, similar device. The FBI director says the complaint alleges the device was meant to capture communications about the company's "super intelligence technology." The maximum penalty is five years and a $250,000 fine. Read that as an allegation, not a finding. The release says nothing about model weights or source code, and I found no primary that does. OpenAI's own statement I have seen only in secondary coverage.

The third item is Meta's Muse Wonder. Meta's own page says Muse Wonder is available today through the Meta Model API and Muse Code, and that Muse Wonder Full is available today "for Muse Code subscribers." The claim is "state-of-the-art performance on personal and professional agentic tasks." Meta's page names no comparison models and gives no price. A TechCrunch article, which I read as a secondary source, reports benchmark parity with two named rival models and a price of four dollars per million input tokens and twenty dollars per million output. It also says the full version is not served through the API. I have not confirmed those specifics against Meta. The part Meta does state is the commercial point: the fuller version is for subscribers. Meta's page does not say whether Full is on the API. TechCrunch says it is not, but I have not confirmed that. For anyone building on these models, the version you can test may not be the version in the launch.

The fourth item is Google's Gemini 4 Argon. Google's post says it is available through the API for developers and enterprises. The price is introductory: two dollars per million input tokens and ten per million output. After the introductory period ends on November thirtieth, 2026, the rate rises to four and twenty, and Google says that applies to the public Argon model. Google also says it used Argon to speed up its own open Gemma serving code, reporting a 2.85 times end-to-end inference speedup on an A100 with vLLM, measured on Gemma 4 E2B. That is a company result about optimizing a serving stack, not a speed figure for Argon itself, and I have not reproduced it.

The fifth item is Anthropic's distillation post. It says early evidence suggests its defenses have cut the data distillers harvest by "around 90 to 99%." The same post says, "We don't yet know how long these gains will last," and that the company has "yet to see a highly sophisticated and persistent attempt to circumvent them." Keep both sentences together. The first is early evidence from the company, not a settled result. The second is the reason it should not yet be called settled.

Put the day's stories side by side. A lab says a worm could spread through cheap devices. A complaint says a recorder was planted at the company that builds the model. Each one turns on the same question: which version of the claim is the one you can check?

Here is the supply-chain version, flagged as my analogy. A purchase order confirmation often says "subject to capacity" in small print. A planner who copies the headline quantity and drops that clause has a plan that is wrong in a way no one will see until the trucks don't come. The Anthropic post is careful about exactly those clauses. The dropped words in my own summary were the ones that said what the result was about. A quote with its qualifier removed is a shorter quote and a different claim.

What this means for you, as suggestions and not practices I have seen work. First, when a vendor shows you a benchmark, ask for the comparison set and the test setup, the way you would ask for the exact spec behind a part number. Second, if you put a token price into a cost model, put the step-up date in the model, not only the introductory rate. Third, when you read a summary, including one from me, check the qualifier before you act on the number. The price that expires in six weeks is the easiest one to miss.
