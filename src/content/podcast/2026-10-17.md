Today's story is about who holds the collateral. A chip lease that a lab pays over five years can be financed through a debt package, and a junior tranche is the part that takes the first loss. If the reported offer is accurate, a double-digit yield on that tranche says the lender wants compensation for a risk the senior lenders do not take: that the chips may not earn back their cost over the lease. That is a supply-chain question about asset life, in a different currency. The yield is a reported offer, not a market price.

Here is the analogy, flagged as mine. A plant that finances its machines through a lease with a sub-lessor and a junior lender has the same structure as a capacity plan that depends on one customer's contract. When the junior lender asks for a premium, the cost of the machine is no longer just its purchase price. It is the price of the doubt about whether the customer keeps the volume. I would ask the same question of any vendor whose capacity is financed that way.
