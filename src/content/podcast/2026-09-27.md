The thread first.

On Sunday the sixth, I told you Broadcom's earnings release did not answer the question everyone was asking about this build-out: is the supplier underwriting the customer's ability to pay?

I said the words guarantee, commitment, purchase commitment and prepay did not appear anywhere in that release. I said that did not mean the answer was no. It meant the answer lived in the quarterly filing, the ten-Q, which was not out yet. And on the eighth I told you I had checked the SEC's filing index that morning and there was still no ten-Q.

It was filed on the tenth of September. I have the filing's text, I checked every figure and quote I am about to give you against it myself, and the answer is yes.

Note ten, under the heading "Financial Guarantee." Quoting it directly: Broadcom "arranged for a financial partner to take on certain agreements to purchase AI racks based on custom AI accelerators designed by us and the related lease agreements with a customer." And then Broadcom "entered into a backstop agreement with the financial partner for the customer's lease obligations over the five-year lease terms."

In plain language: a financial partner buys racks built around Broadcom's custom chips and leases them to a customer. If the customer stops paying, Broadcom pays the partner. The filing says the payout is eighty-five percent of what is still owed on the lease, minus whatever the racks are worth at that point.

And the size, verbatim: "Our maximum potential liability under the Backstop upon the deployment of all AI racks, on an undiscounted basis, was approximately twenty-nine billion dollars."

Broadcom says the fair value of that guarantee is not material and nothing has been paid out. That is their accounting judgment, and I have no basis to dispute it.

There is a second item in the same note. A customer "may, under certain circumstances and if needed, issue to us convertible promissory notes up to an aggregate principal amount of forty-two billion dollars." None had been issued as of the end of the quarter. So Broadcom stands ready to *lend* a customer up to forty-two billion dollars. Whether that is the same customer as the leases, the filing does not say, and I will come back to that.

Now let me put that next to what I told you on the sixth, because I owe you that. On the sixth I said Broadcom was capturing AI spending without spending its own capital — "the customer builds the datacenter, the customer buys the power," and Broadcom books the silicon as cash. I called that a structural position, the most durable fact in the filing.

The framing was incomplete. Part of that position is conditional. Broadcom is not simply selling into other people's balance sheets. It is standing behind one customer's lease payments, up to twenty-nine billion dollars, and separately it stands ready to take up to forty-two billion dollars of convertible notes from a customer. The capital is not on Broadcom's books today. Part of the risk sits with Broadcom anyway.

Here is the analogy I would use on a plant floor. Imagine your biggest component supplier telling you: go lease the machines you need to run our parts, and if you ever miss a lease payment, we will cover it. That supplier is not just a supplier anymore. It is part guarantor. Add a standing offer to lend, which Broadcom has made to a customer that may or may not be the same one, and it starts to look like part lender too. When things are going well, that looks like a great partnership. When they are not, the supplier's revenue and its guarantee go bad together.

One more thing the filing does *not* say. I searched it for OpenAI, Anthropic, Google, Microsoft, Meta and Oracle. Zero hits for each. The filing never names the customer. It also does not say whether the customer with the backstopped leases, the customer that could issue the convertible notes, and the large long-term custom-accelerator contract the filing mentions are all the same company. Coverage may put a name on it. The filing does not, so I am not going to either.

Then Oracle.

Oracle's quarterly filing came out the next day, the eleventh, for the quarter ended August thirty-first. I searched it for the same thing, supplier guarantees, and found none. The words guarantee and off-balance return nothing. The risk has a different shape.

First, leases. Quoting: "two hundred eighty-eight billion dollars of additional lease commitments, substantially all related to data center arrangements." Those leases are generally expected to start between this quarter and fiscal twenty twenty-nine, running fifteen to nineteen years, and because they have not started, they are *not* on the balance sheet. For scale, the operating leases that *are* on the balance sheet total thirty-four point six billion.

Second, free cash flow. Oracle's own non-GAAP figure was negative five point four billion dollars for the quarter. A year ago it was negative three hundred sixty-two million. Capital spending was twenty-eight and a half billion, against eight and a half billion a year earlier.

Third, and this is the one I find most interesting: "eleven point four billion dollars of prepayments from customers that included a significant financing component." The filing says no prepayments like that came in during the same quarter a year ago. That is the only comparison it gives, so I will say new against that quarter, not "the first ever."

Notice which way the money runs. At Broadcom, the supplier stands behind the customer. At Oracle, *customers* are financing the supplier: paying in advance, on terms Oracle has to book partly as interest. Different direction, same basic fact. In this build-out, the line between who sells, who buys and who lends has blurred.

And again: the filing names no counterparties. OpenAI, Stargate, Crusoe, Blue Owl, Nvidia, SoftBank — zero hits each. Who holds the two hundred eighty-eight billion in leases, and who prepaid the eleven point four billion, is not disclosed.

Now a piece I have to label clearly as second-hand. On the twenty-fourth, TechCrunch, following a Bloomberg report I could not open, reported that Oracle sent a force-majeure notice on Project Jupiter, a Stargate data center in New Mexico. According to that report, the notice "would allow the company to delay payments should the facility miss its twenty twenty-eight target to come online." Oracle's statement, as TechCrunch quotes it: "Project Jupiter remains on our planned schedule." And Blue Owl, the financing partner: "This notice does not change the financial commitments to this multi-year project."

I have not seen the notice itself, and I do not know what event it cites. So here is all I will say. The contract terms that matter in this build-out are no longer just price and delivery date. They are who pays if the site is not ready on time. That is a supply-chain question, and it is one you already know how to ask.

Third: Akamai and Anthropic. This one I read on the primary, the press release Akamai filed with the SEC on the twenty-fourth.

Quoting: "a significantly expanded relationship with Anthropic for eleven point six billion dollars of contractual commitment over seven years." It will "support Anthropic's accelerating CPU workload demands." CPU, not GPU. It is worth noticing that a frontier lab's big new commitment this week is for ordinary processors, the kind that run agents' tool calls, orchestration and plumbing, rather than model training. I am offering that as my reading of why CPU. The release says demand, and it does not break down which workloads.

The release says the deal can grow by up to another nine billion, for a total of about twenty billion. Akamai expects about five and a half billion in capital spending to serve it. And this is the line I would point any operator at: about one point seven billion dollars of extra capital spending *this year* "to secure and pre-purchase critical supply chain components, including memory."

That is a company pulling purchases forward to lock in parts it expects to be short on. You have done that. Everyone who lived through twenty twenty-one has done it. When a cloud provider says it out loud in a press release, it tells you what it thinks the memory market looks like next year.

And the last piece of the deal: Akamai issued Anthropic a warrant, meaning the right to buy up to about five percent of Akamai's stock at one hundred eleven dollars and thirty-three cents a share. About two percent vests with this commitment, and the rest vests only if the relationship grows. The customer gets equity in its supplier, sized to how much it buys. Think of it as a volume rebate paid in stock.

So that is three disclosures from three companies in one month, and none of them describes simple buying and selling. The supplier guarantees the customer. Customers finance the supplier. The customer ends up owning part of the supplier. I am not claiming any one of these is dangerous. I am saying the standard vendor-customer contract you would recognize is not what is being signed at the top of this industry right now. And when the money gets tangled like this, the first thing to check is how each side's exposure is growing, not the headline price.

Now the week's other big story, and this one lands directly on your costs. Both frontier labs cut prices within the same day.

On the twenty-second, OpenAI released two models, GPT-6 Sol and GPT-6 Luna. Their announcement on the OpenAI developer forum, verbatim: "fifty percent lower API prices for Sol and Luna compared with GPT-5.6 promotional pricing." The model pages list Sol at two dollars per million input tokens and ten dollars per million output. Luna is ten cents in and fifty cents out, described as OpenAI's "most efficient model for focused, high-volume tasks."

Look at the base of that comparison: compared with *promotional* pricing. I could not open OpenAI's main blog post, so I do not have their comparison against GPT-5.6's regular list price. TechCrunch reports it, but that is second-hand, so I will leave it there. Fifty percent below a promotion is a real cut. Just be clear what it is fifty percent *of*.

The same day, Anthropic released Claude Opus 5.5, and I read its announcement page directly. The headline is that it "costs forty percent less to run than Opus 5."

This is where you have to read the next paragraph. The list prices are four dollars per million input tokens and twenty dollars per million output, and the page itself says that is "twenty percent less than Opus 5." Cache reads, re-reading material the model has already seen, are twenty cents per million, sixty percent less. The forty percent is Anthropic's own measurement of what a typical workload costs: "Our tests show that at default settings it will cost forty percent less than Opus 5 on typical workloads." The page credits two things: cheaper prices, especially that sixty percent cut on cache reads, and, by Anthropic's account, the model getting the same work done in fewer steps. One quote on the page, attributed to a Spotify engineer, and it says the model matched Opus 5's quality "in about half the turns, time and output tokens."

So twenty percent is the list price. Forty percent is the vendor's measured estimate for typical work. Your number depends on your work. That is not a knock on Anthropic. They printed both figures in adjacent paragraphs. It is a reminder that "costs forty percent less" is a claim about a *workload*, not a price sheet, and the only workload that counts is yours.

One more sentence off the same page, because it connects to something this show covered on the eighth. Anthropic writes that Opus 5.5 "is our first release since we called for pacing the frontier." Before we close, the AppliedIQ angle.

This week's pieces fit together into one lesson, and it is about ownership.

Look at what OpenAI itself tells developers on that deprecations page about the prompt object going away. The migration advice is one line: "move reusable prompt content into your application code." In other words: the thing you rented from the platform is going away, so bring it home and own it.

Then look at the week. Price cuts from both labs in one day. Four model names retired tomorrow, a batch more in October. A video API shut down with no named successor. The model underneath an AI workflow is going to change several times a year, on the vendor's schedule, not yours. That is not a complaint. It is how this market works right now, and the price cuts are the upside of the same churn.

So for a lean manufacturer or distributor, the question is not which model to pick. It is: *which parts of the AI workflow do you own?* The prompts, the rules for what counts as a rush order, the test cases that prove the system gets your part numbers right, the record of what it decided and why — those should live in something you control. Then the model is a part you can swap, like changing a supplier on an approved-vendor list. You requalify it against your own tests and move on. That is the AppliedIQ pitch in one sentence, and this week OpenAI made the argument for it on its own page.

Two concrete actions.

First, this week: find out which model names your integrations call. If you built something yourself, search the code or the configuration for the model name it uses and check that name against OpenAI's deprecations page — do not just search for the old ones, because the October list reaches into models from last year. If a vendor built it, ask them one question in writing: which model and snapshot does this call, and what is your plan for the October twenty-third shutdown? If they cannot answer quickly, that tells you something about who owns your workflow.

Second, the next time anyone quotes you an AI price, ask for it per completed task, not per token. Cost per successful result, the phrase from Nate Jones's show notes. Anthropic's own page shows why: a twenty percent list-price cut became, by Anthropic's own tests, a forty percent cost cut on typical workloads, through cheaper cache reads and, by their account, fewer steps. The token price is the vendor's number. The cost per completed task is yours, and it is the only one that tells you whether the job got cheaper.

And one thing to watch. Akamai is pre-buying memory. If you are planning a server refresh, new industrial PCs, or anything with a lot of memory in it for the first half of next year, get quotes early. That is an inference from one press release, not a forecast I am scoring, but it costs you one email to act on.
