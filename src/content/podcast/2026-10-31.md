The license is the part that matters for anyone who would deploy the model. The file's own header reads: "This license is a modified version of the MIT license." The modification is a condition. Section 2 says: "If the Licensee or any of its affiliates operates a Model as a Service business, the Licensee must enter into a separate commercial license with Mistral before using the Model or its derivative works for any commercial purpose."

The license defines the operative term as "giving a third party access to the Model or its derivative works for inference or post-training by any means". The test then turns on whether that outside party can exercise "meaningful control over the inputs, parameters, or training data." The license carves out two cases from that definition: "end-user products with model capabilities solely embedded within specific features or harnesses," and "mere relaying of requests to models hosted by others." Section 3 separately exempts "internal use of the Model or its derivative works," which it defines as "any use that does not make the Model or its derivative works available to third parties". A second exemption covers "any use of the Model accessed through Mistral’s official products."

Taken plainly, an organization that runs the model for its own staff falls under the internal-use exemption, and the commercial-license clause does not apply. That reading holds only if no outside party, such as a supplier or customer portal, can reach the model. The notice condition in Section 1 is separate. As written, it requires that "The above copyright notice and this permission notice shall be included in all copies or substantial portions of the Model", and the Section 3 exemption does not remove it, so I would keep the notice with any copy I deployed. An organization that gives outside customers inference access through its own API or a hosted endpoint, where those customers control the inputs, is the case that needs a separate commercial license from Mistral. The middle case is where I would not give a confident answer. A product that embeds the model inside a feature may fall inside the exclusion for embedded capabilities, and the word "solely" in that exclusion is doing real work. That is a question for counsel reading the whole license against the exact product, not for me reading one section.

Here is the supply-chain version of the Nscale story. A tier-one supplier co-signs the loan a tier-two supplier takes out to build a plant that will make the tier-one's parts. If the tier-two defaults on construction, the tier-one steps in, takes over the plant, and finds another operator to run it. The tier-one has not bought more parts. It has taken on the risk that the capacity it schedules around gets built at all.

That is what the residual value guarantee does, and it is why this story belongs in an operator's notebook even though nothing is signed. When a manufacturer's supply depends on a partner's capacity, the question is who holds the risk if that partner's build slips. In these reports, a proposed arrangement would move that risk for the Anthropic campus toward the chip vendor. Nscale's own record gives a reason to ask. The chips it had running in August were a small share of its plan, and the Microsoft pullout in England shows that a planned site can slip for years when its power arrives late.

For a manufacturing client thinking about hosting a model, the Mistral license is the term to read first, the way you would read the terms on a supplier's drawing package before you build on it. Running the model for your own planners falls under the internal-use exemption in the terms I reviewed. Giving outside customers inference access through an API or hosted endpoint, with control over inputs, is the case that needs a separate commercial license. Embedding the model inside a product is the grey area, and I would have counsel read that clause against the exact product before anyone commits budget. I would not tell a client that the open weights are free to use commercially without that check.
