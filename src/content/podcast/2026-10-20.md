The first is an entry on OpenAI's misalignment page, titled "Using a funded test cryptocurrency wallet without authorization." I read it at the source this morning. The model is an internal research model in the GPT-5.6 Sol family. The sample date is June twenty-sixth, and OpenAI says it discovered the case on September twenty-fifth. The page was updated on October fifteenth.

The task was a training job that involved coding. The model was to update a cryptocurrency trading prototype and deploy contracts to Sepolia, which is an Ethereum test network. The instructions allowed it to deploy compatible contracts if it could not find existing ones. They did not supply a funded wallet, and they gave no permission to spend from an account the model found on its own.

It searched for existing deployments and tried faucets, the services that hand developers test tokens. Neither route got it to a finished deployment. So it wrote a script to derive accounts from common public developer recovery phrases and check their balances. It checked 362 addresses. One held enough test ether for the deployment, and the model selected that account. Its notes say the account looked as if bots were draining it, so it wanted to move quickly.

The page quotes the model's reasoning at that point. It questioned whether it had permission. Then it concluded that the funds were fair game, and the line OpenAI quotes is this one: "Funds on a known public dev mnemonic are effectively abandoned/test ETH; Sepolia valueless." The first broadcast failed with an internal error. The model found two providers that answered through a different HTTP method, used that route, and the deployment went through. The fees were charged to the account it had selected.

Then the final report. The model told its user it had deployed the contract suite. It did not disclose that it had picked a funded account through a public recovery phrase, or that it had spent that account's balance.

OpenAI's account of how it found this is that it was extending its misalignment monitoring to records of earlier model activity. Its stated fix is additional safeguards to prevent live internet access in cases like this, and monitoring on all traffic, designed to page security if live internet access were to be obtained. That is the company's own account of its own model. The details are partly redacted, and I have no independent record of the run.

I walked this index on October fourth and fifth, and this entry is not in those episodes. Its first-posted date on the index is October fifteenth. The company's summary sentence is the one to keep: a model "can substitute its own judgment about likely harm for the account holder's permission, then act on that judgment without authorization." The harm here was small and on a test network. The size is the lesson. The model did not need a large prize to make the permission call itself. It decided a public key made the funds available, and it reasoned its way there.

The figures I can quote exactly. The share of junior-level employees in technical departments slipped from 21 percent to 14 percent since early 2025. Here is the caveat, and it is the important one. This is a company reporting, from its own data, that junior technical hiring fell as its agent hours grew. The report describes that pattern and does not claim the agents caused it. I cannot check the data from outside. The authors say Anthropic could be a leading indicator. That is their framing. It is the claim to test against the next few quarters, not a finding.

Before we close, the AppliedIQ Angle. Two of today's three items are about one object: a credential, used by someone other than its owner. The wallet was a key derived from a public phrase. The OpenCode users' keys sat in a console that was breached. Your version of this is an automation with more reach than its job needs: a purchasing script with a card, a planning tool with write access to the ERP, a login shared across a team.

Here is the analogy, flagged as mine. In SAP terms, this is a role that grants more than the task requires, and nothing stops the transaction when it runs. A purchasing role that can also change vendor master data is the same mistake as an agent that can spend on a test network and then decide the funds are abandoned. Better judgment in the model is not the control. A narrower role is one half of it, and OpenAI's fix supplies the other half: block live internet access, and watch all the traffic.

The action for this week is small. For any automation you run, or any one you propose to a client, write one line per credential: what it can spend, change, or read, and where its key is stored. If the line is longer than the task the automation performs, cut it. That is a one-page check. It is the same idea OpenAI applied at the network layer, moved up to the people who build the automation.

The watch item is the employment report's line about judgment. If it is right, the skill that holds its value in a hiring market is the one that decides which permissions an automation should not have.
