Before we close, the AppliedIQ angle.

Put the day side by side. Broadcom is a supplier, a lender and a possible shareholder of a lab, and the lab's prospectus, as relayed, says that dual role could affect its access to compute. The federal government is a buyer, a referee and, in one interview answer, a possible owner. And OpenAI's own documentation says the approval gate on its API browser is per website, that a confirmation the agent has to call works only if the agent calls it, and that where a purchase must be confirmed, you should keep the browser away from anything that can make one, or run a browser you control. My reading: the control has to sit outside the agent. Three different situations, one question: is the check independent of the thing being checked?

Your language for that is segregation of duties. In an ERP, the person who creates the vendor does not approve the payment, and the reason is not that anyone is dishonest. It is that a control which depends on the person being controlled is not a control. Broadcom's two roles, a possible owner who is also a referee, and the browser passage each describe a check tied to what it checks, or one that could become so. The FTC investigation and the California subpoena are the opposite: they are what an independent check looks like. The question to ask of any vendor is which kind of check sits between you and the risk.

One concrete action, and two quick ones.

The action: write a one-page "second key" table into every AppliedIQ agent offer. Three columns. The action class, meaning anything that moves money, changes master data, or sends something outside the company. The named human who approves it. And where the approval is recorded, in a place the agent cannot write to. Never a function the agent calls, and never an automated reply. That is my reading of what OpenAI's page implies, and it is the line a client's auditor or insurer will eventually ask for. It also continues the hard cap from the first of October: the cap says when the agent stops, and the second key says who restarts it.

Two quick ones. First, add a financing-and-dependency line to your vendor checklist: who supplies this vendor, who lends to it, who regulates it. You do that for a sole-source component already. Second, before any client moves from Astra to Sol on a price one-fifth as high, rerun ten of that client's real assignments on both, count the finished tasks and the retries, and price the finished task. That is the idea in Nate's notes; the ten, and counting the retries, are mine. A lower per-token price does not answer it.

That is my suggestion, not a fact about your market.
