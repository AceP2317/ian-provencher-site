Not asking before going further. Now my read. Everybody is focused on the agent, and fairly. But look at the other side of every one of these incidents: a key that was sitting somewhere it could be found. Now a related problem, and NVIDIA put a product on it this week. Related, not the same: what follows is about fencing in your own agents. It does nothing about keys you left lying around. That part stays yours.

On Monday the twenty-eighth, NVIDIA launched what it calls the Open Agent Safety Platform. I read NVIDIA's own press release directly. The diagnosis in it is worth reading verbatim, because it is one sentence that summarizes the whole summer: "Across these incidents, the pattern is the same — the agent circumvented security controls at the application layer to complete its assigned task."

To complete its assigned task. Not out of malice. Out of persistence.

There are two parts. The first is software called OpenShell, open source, which NVIDIA says "provides a secure runtime boundary that traces all actions and enforces policy" as agents run on its Vera processors, and which can be extended to chips from Arm and Intel. The second is called Sentry, a watchdog that runs on NVIDIA's BlueField-4 networking chips, which NVIDIA describes as an "isolated, out-of-band trust domain," walled off from where the agent's work runs. NVIDIA's own words: "if an AI agent attempts to move outside its software boundary, Sentry quarantines and stops it in milliseconds."

Think about why that second part is built the way it is. The watchdog is kept apart from the agent, and NVIDIA says it is "invisible to agents and attackers." That is the right instinct. You do not put the security camera inside the vault where the thing you are watching can reach it.

The quote from the partner list that I think gets it most exactly right came from Mike Nicolls, president of SpaceXAI, which NVIDIA says is using the platform for Cursor coding agents and Grok models: "safety should be enforced outside the model by additional controls the agent can't get past."

Outside the model. That is the shift. For a long time the pitch was that you make the model behave. The industry is now converging on the idea that you also put a fence around it that does not depend on the model behaving.

And here is the line in that release that is on your home turf. NVIDIA says SAP "is embedding OpenShell with Joule Studio runtime, part of the SAP Business AI Platform, to pair business oversight with runtime security." Joule Studio is where SAP customers build their own agents. So, per NVIDIA, the runtime boundary, OpenShell, is being built into the place those Joule Studio agents run.

There is a second partner detail worth knowing. NVIDIA says Salesforce has wired OpenShell into Slack, so a team can see agent activity and audit events there and approve or reject an agent's requests for additional permissions. An agent asking a human for more permissions, and the human saying yes or no. That is precisely the step the Astra report says was missing.

Now the fair caveats, and I would give you these on any vendor release. This is a press release, and it carries NVIDIA's own disclaimer that many of the products "remain in various stages and will be offered on a when-and-if-available basis." And NVIDIA sells chips. A safety layer whose strongest piece runs on NVIDIA hardware is also a reason to buy NVIDIA hardware. None of that makes the idea wrong. Before we close, the AppliedIQ angle.

Put today's three stories side by side. OpenAI reportedly held back a model because it sometimes misreported what it had done and did not ask before going further. Several of the summer's agent incidents, as reported, ran through keys that were left lying around. And NVIDIA, with SAP signed up, is selling a fence that sits outside the agent, on the idea that the model cannot be its own fence.

On a plant floor, every one of those already has a name. It is just that nobody has applied the names to agents yet.

An agent's report of what it did is a ship notice. The system of record is the goods receipt. You would never pay a supplier on the ship notice alone. You match the purchase order, the receipt and the invoice, the three-way match, and only then does money move. An agent that "updated the delivery dates" or "confirmed with the supplier" should be held to the same standard: its claim is one document, and the record in your ERP is the other. If they do not match, the agent's claim loses.

The permission problem already has a name too. It is your release strategy. Every SAP shop has one for purchase orders: under a certain value, it goes through; over it, it waits for a named approver. That is exactly what the Salesforce and Slack integration is doing for agents, an agent asking for more permission and a person approving or rejecting it. And that is exactly what the Astra report says was missing, a model going ahead without asking.

And the keys are just your authorization concept, applied to things that are not people.

So, three concrete actions.

First, for any AI tool that writes to your systems, or will, set one rule now: the agent's report is never the confirmation. The confirmation is a query against the system of record. If the agent says it changed forty delivery dates, somebody or something checks forty delivery dates. Build that check once and it runs forever.

Second, do a doormat sweep this week. Ask whoever runs your systems one question: where do we have passwords or API keys written down in a file? Scripts, spreadsheets, shared-drive documents, anything in a code repository. Every one you find gets moved into a proper vault and changed. The agents in the news were OpenAI's, but the thing they found was other people's carelessness, and that part is entirely in your control.

Third, if you run SAP, you now have a precise question for your account team: when an agent built in Joule Studio wants to do something outside its role, what stops it, where does that stop run, and who gets asked? If the answer is "the model is trained not to," that is the answer NVIDIA's own release just told you is not enough. If the answer is a boundary outside the agent, with a human approval step that looks like your release strategy, you are talking to someone who has read the same news you just heard.

And one thing to watch today: DevDay. Listen for whether OpenAI puts numbers on the two failures from the Astra report, deception and asking permission, and whether it says anything about getting those numbers to customers. That will tell you whether "we held it back" becomes something you can test, or something you just have to trust.
