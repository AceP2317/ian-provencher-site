First: OpenAI published 719 AI-generated math manuscripts.

Sourcing. I read the README of the repository OpenAI put up, github dot com, slash openai, slash math. I did not get OpenAI's own blog post. It returned a refusal from here, so anything the post adds, I have not seen. I did not read the manuscripts themselves, and I did not check any proof.

What the page says, verbatim: "The current catalogue contains 719 manuscripts organized into 372 families." And: "The vast majority of results were obtained with the same procedure using an unreleased internal OpenAI model." The model was posed, in the README's words, "approximately 4,000 problems." And then the sentence I would underline: "Some of the unformalized results could have issues." The README also says about 42 percent of its top-line results are formalized, meaning checked by a proof assistant, not just written up.

What is not on the page. Press coverage gives the count as 722. The repository I read says 719, and I used that. The page does not name the model. It does not support the claim, in some coverage, that a single prompt produced the work. The press figures of 722 and of 162 papers formalized in Lean do not appear in the README.

My read, flagged as mine. For a listener who does not do mathematics, the distinction is this. A written proof is a human-readable argument, and a flaw can hide in it for years. A formalized result has been run through a proof assistant, a program that checks every step and refuses the ones it cannot verify. It checks the formal statement, step by step. It does not check that the statement matches the problem the paper meant. The 42 percent is the share of top-line results the README says has passed that check. The rest has not been run through it, and the README says those results sit at different stages of verification.

The number that matters is not the 719. It is the 58 percent of top-line results with no proof assistant behind them. The authors are candid that some of it may be wrong, and that is the honest part. But a catalogue this size only helps a reader who knows which of its entries to trust, and the repository, as I read it, does not say how to tell.

Before we close, the AppliedIQ Angle.

Put the five items side by side. OpenAI produced hundreds of manuscripts, and about four in ten of its top-line results carry a proof behind them. Mistral and Reflection each produced a model whose weights are not yet out. Wikimedia, as The Register reports it, says agents edited its wikis without authorization. And SAP is building plant-floor agents that resolve disruptions as they happen. Each item is a production story with a checking story underneath it. That is the thread I said I would pull, and it is my read, not a finding.

The supply-chain version, flagged as my analogy. Imagine a receiving dock that counts forty-two percent of its pallets against the packing list and waves the rest through. The dock is not lazy. It is fast. But the variance you do not see is the one that lands in the planning run. The 58 percent of top-line results without a proof behind them is that dock.

On SAP, the analogy I would use with a planner is the exception report. In MRP, the exception list is how a planner learns that a safety stock or a lead time is wrong. Each exception that gets handled quietly is a parameter that stays wrong. Doehler's CIO says the goal is processes that are "self-resolving rather than exception-driven." That may be the right goal for routine disruptions. The planner still needs the record of what was resolved, and why, to know whether the master data is right. A Manufacturing Assistant that fixes things without leaving a trail is a faster way to keep a bad parameter.

What this means for you, as suggestions and not practices I have seen work. First, in any pilot with an agent that acts on your systems, ask the vendor three things in writing: what the agent is allowed to change, what it is not allowed to touch, and where the record of each action lives. The Wikimedia item is the reason. The word "unauthorized" is the one a buyer should be asking about, and an agent that cannot say what it was allowed to do cannot tell you whether it stayed inside that.

Second, ask for the exception log, not just the resolved count. Any vendor pitch will lead with how many disruptions the assistant handled. Ask instead how many it handled that a planner would have wanted to see, and whether those are listed somewhere the planner can open.

Third, for a lean shop, the question to ask about any model claim is the one the OpenAI README gives a figure for: what share is formalized, meaning checked by a proof assistant. A forty-two percent figure on a stated basis is more useful to a buyer than a catalogue size with none. Ask the vendor for the checked share of whatever they are selling you, and ask how they measure it.

Fourth, and this one you can do without a vendor. Pick the one report your planner trusts least, and count its exceptions for four weeks. Write down each one, whether it was real, and what the master data said. Then you have a measured checked share for your own shop, which is the number every vendor on this list should be asked to beat. It costs a spreadsheet and a month, and it is the kind of baseline a pilot needs before anyone can say the agent helped.
