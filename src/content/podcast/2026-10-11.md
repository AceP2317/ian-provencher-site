First: Cloudflare says the Deno team is joining Cloudflare.

Sourcing. I read the Cloudflare post dated the ninth of October, at blog dot cloudflare dot com, slash deno-joins-cloudflare. Two authors signed it, Ryan Dahl and Kenton Varda. The line that matters is in the first paragraph: "The Deno team is joining Cloudflare, and we're merging workerd and celld!" Workerd is Cloudflare's Workers runtime. Deno is the JavaScript and TypeScript runtime Dahl created. The post names Dahl and Bert Belder as leading the effort to make self-hosting "a first-class supported way" to run Workers apps.

What the post does not say: price, closing terms, or headcount. The page carries an acquisitions tag, but the text never uses the word acquisition, so I will not use it either. Call it a combination announced by the buyer, with the terms undisclosed.

The part I did not finish. The post defers the future of the Deno runtime to a separate Deno post. I did not open it. A secondary aggregator claims a Deno runtime end-of-life in October 2027 and a Deno Deploy shutdown in April 2027. I found nothing in the primary coverage that supports those dates, so I am not airing them.

My read, flagged as opinion. The signal in this post is the self-hosting sentence, not the logo. A vendor does not usually promise to make it easy to leave its platform unless it knows that leaving is the first objection a buyer raises. Read that sentence as an admission about lock-in, and as a stated intention you can check them against. Whether it holds is the thing to watch.

Before we close, the AppliedIQ Angle.

Put the two infrastructure stories side by side. One is a runtime consolidating under a larger vendor and promising an exit path. The other is a model that can reach websites and forms it was never meant to touch, from yesterday, and a false tip that a police department's intake stopped before it was investigated. Both are about the same question, which is who controls the place where the work runs, and who can leave it.

The supply-chain version, flagged as my analogy. A plant that runs its planning on one vendor's system and one vendor's hosting has a single point of failure, even when the software works perfectly. The Cloudflare sentence about self-hosting is the equivalent of a vendor saying the plant can keep its own data and run the planning on its own servers. It only matters if somebody has tested it. A supplier who says the exit exists is a claim to verify before you need it, not a comfort.

What this means for you, as suggestions and not practices I have seen work. First, if you build on a hosted runtime for anything AppliedIQ delivers to a client, write down which parts you could run on your own hardware, and which would break. Second, ask any vendor whose agent can submit forms or reach outside services what the stop control is, and who can read the log of what it did. Third, treat the Gemini 4 Argon question as a watch item, not a purchase. The introductory price is already published, at two dollars in and ten out per million tokens, rising to four and twenty. The thing to check is whether it holds and what the data terms are, and the benchmark numbers will not answer either one.

I have not verified that any of these vendors' exit promises will hold, and I have not tested Cloudflare's self-hosting path. Treat the self-hosting sentence as a question to check, not a fact.
