It carries a post dated October 26 announcing that the company has filed an S-1 with the SEC for its proposed public offering. The second item is an encryption result. Two technology sites reported on November 6 that Google DeepMind used AI agents to find weaknesses in 1024-bit RSA, an encryption standard that older systems still rely on. The reports describe a way to forge signatures under that scheme faster than had been thought. One summary gives a large speedup figure for a narrow case and calls it theoretical. One summary says keys of 2048 bits and longer are not affected. I have not checked that. I could not open the academic paper, and I could not open a DeepMind post on the subject. The DeepMind listing I could read is only the first page of a long index, and it has no item about RSA. The stored copy of the other listing is unreadable. So the claim rests on two secondary reports, and I am not treating its size as settled.

Here is the useful line for the systems you run. A vendor saying that the cost of serving its product was unsustainably high relative to its revenue is telling you something about pricing. If a company cannot yet serve its product at a margin, a subscription to that product may be priced below its cost, and the price can move when that changes. I have one unopened report for this one company, so that is a question to ask, not a finding. Before you commit to an AI tool in a plant or an office, ask the vendor two things in writing: what its usage limits are, and what happens to your price if those limits are cut or the price is raised. That costs nothing to ask, and it is the question the Grok story makes you need to answer.

The encryption item is a different kind of question for your shop. If your customers' quotes, purchase orders or signed documents depend on an older signature scheme, the question to put to your IT provider is simple: which signing standard and RSA key length your documents use, and when that was last checked? I have no finding about any particular system. I am raising it because the report, if it holds, is about the cryptography your signatures depend on, not the software around them.
