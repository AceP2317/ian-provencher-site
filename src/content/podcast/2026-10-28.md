Today's lead is a company memo, not a launch. Microsoft confirmed to TechCrunch on Monday that it is offering buyouts to around sixty thousand employees. The note from its chief people officer, Amy Coleman, was also published on the company's website. Eligible staff are vice presidents and below on select teams in the United States, Canada, India and the United Kingdom. Bloomberg, as TechCrunch reports it, names Xbox, Windows, Copilot and Finance as participating divisions. Some employees, including those in data centers, sales and marketing, are excluded. The terms are similar to Microsoft's standard severance.

Scale it against the headcount. Microsoft's headcount at the end of June was 223,000, so around sixty thousand is a little over a quarter of the company. That is the reported offer size. TechCrunch does not report how many staff fall inside the eligibility rules, which are set by level, team and geography. The note calls the offer new for the company and says Microsoft will learn from it. TechCrunch reads the offer as a way to cut headcount without mass layoffs. For an operator, the useful part is that the reduction is framed as a choice rather than a layoff. Secondary coverage I could not open directly, which a search summary attributes to GeekWire and CNBC, adds that employees have until early December to decide and that exits take effect on December thirty-first. The same summary attributes a line to a spokesperson that AI is not what is driving the program. Treat all three as reported, not confirmed.

Earlier this year Microsoft offered buyouts to retirement-age employees. TechCrunch says more than 30 percent of roughly 8,750 eligible workers accepted. Whether the roles in this round are being replaced by AI tooling or simply carried at lower cost, the public record does not say yet. That is the question to keep open until the company's own statements or filings answer it.

The second item rests on TechCrunch's report of Google's announcement, and I did not store Google's own post. Google DeepMind released Genie 4 on Tuesday in research preview for what TechCrunch calls trusted testers. Genie is a world model: you type a prompt and it generates an interactive world you can move through. This version generates real-time audio that matches the scene, including music, ambient noise and interactive dialogue. Users can type prompts mid-simulation to change the scene, such as the weather or the camera angle, or to add people and objects. It holds a consistent scene for up to two minutes, double its predecessor's limit. Gemini sits in the loop as a director that plans steps and checks frames while Genie creates the visuals.

The limits matter as much as the features. Google's team said details and objects can drift the longer a simulation runs, and that they have not worked out how to make the audio accurate or in-world text legible. Diego Rivas, a Google DeepMind engineer, stressed that it is still early days and that the end-to-end product is not yet clear. Rivas also pointed to where the real-time approach is most valuable: ideation and storyboarding. TechCrunch reports that trusted testers see film prototyping as a possible new use. Those are the testers' and the engineer's views, not a Google product plan. TechCrunch reports Google's rollout plan as AI Ultra subscribers in Project Genie, and early-access partners of the Gemini API and Google AI Studio, in the coming weeks.
