Today's lead is a model Mistral says you can download. Mistral has released the open weights of Mistral Large 4, which it calls Le Chonk. I read Mistral's post today. Its own sentence is this: "We release the weights of Le Chonk, advancing the frontier of open models in agentic coding and cybersecurity." The post describes a one-trillion-parameter model that is natively multimodal. It says the model was in public preview earlier this month, and that you can download the weights from Hugging Face and deploy them on your own infrastructure with vLLM or SGLang.

The post I read has no date of its own. I have not checked the Hugging Face repository myself, so the download is Mistral's claim until I do. The post reports its own benchmark figures, and cites Artificial Analysis and Surge Code. I have not verified any of them, so no figure appears in this episode.

The post also discloses a safety finding. It says that it "observed the model refusing to comply with black-box attacks against live targets," but that "we see a potential for uplift to conduct real attacks." It adds, "We are modifying our guardrails accordingly," and the sentence runs on past the part I stored. Anyone who would put this model near a security team should weigh that before they deploy it.

The license matters more than the benchmarks for anyone who would deploy it. The post says the model "is available to use under the Mistral Ecosystem Open-Weight license, a modified MIT license." The word "modified" is the clause to watch. I have read the post's description of the license, not the license text, and a summary of a license is not the license. The post also says a business that runs the model as a Model-as-a-Service offering "must obtain a separate commercial license from Mistral before using the model commercially." Anyone who puts this model into a client's stack should read the text first.

Third, the Anthropic filing. The company's own announcement says the price range has not been set, so the first number that matters is the one the filing eventually gives. Until then, the ticker is a date, not a valuation.

A set of open weights is a bit like a drawing package from a supplier. You get the design, but the purchase terms decide what you may build, sell and change. A "modified" license is a clause, and clauses are where the surprises sit. The post's condition for a managed service is exactly that kind of clause. If you are advising a manufacturer on putting a model on its own servers, put the license on the list of documents you read before the architecture, not after. If a client wants to offer the model to its own customers as a service, get the commercial terms in writing before the design is signed off.

The introductory price is the same trap as a post-promotion supplier quote. The number in the first month is not the cost you will live with. Build the cost at the list price, then see what the discount does to your margin on the calls you actually make. If you cannot make the case at list, the promotion is not a reason to buy.

For a supply-chain buyer, the Anthropic announcement is a useful lesson in reading a notice of intent. It tells you a company has started a process. It does not tell you the terms of the transaction. Ask the same question of any supplier's announcement: what is the number, and when will it be set?
