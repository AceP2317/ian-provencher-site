Yesterday's episode said the public filing would settle the compute total if it gave one. It does. Anthropic's amended S-1, filed on EDGAR on November 2, is the company's own disclosure, and I checked the passages that matter against the full text. I will go through what it says first, then the arithmetic I did on it, then what I could not confirm.

The first number is the total. The filing says Anthropic has purchase commitments and lease agreements, including short-term commitments and leases not yet commenced, with an aggregate committed spend of approximately $895.4 billion, "substantially all of which is related to compute and infrastructure." About $447.5 billion of that is expected to be incurred before December 31, 2030, and substantially all of the remainder between 2031 and 2045. Approximately $69.8 billion is due in the 12 months after September 30, 2026. That is the company's own figure. The filing also says that approximately $213.8 billion of the total is in arrangements with exercisable termination rights or contingent conditions before they start, and $4.3 billion of the $69.8 billion due in 12 months is in the same position.

The second number is the quarter. Revenue rose from $197 million in the three months ended December 31, 2024, to $17.0 billion in the three months ended September 30, 2026. The operating loss for the September quarter was $567 million. The June quarter had operating income of $974 million, so the swing is large. The filing attributes the loss mainly to two things. The first is $5.0 billion of compute under short-term commitments, which, in the filing's words, "costs significantly more than comparable compute procured" under its multi-year commitments. The second is $2.0 billion of non-cash charges tied to its donation matching program. Adjusted operating income for the quarter was $2.5 billion, and the adjusted operating margin fell to 15 percent from 19 percent in the June quarter.

The filing's own explanation is about how it buys compute. The majority of its compute is contracted under multi-year commitments that it typically procures 12 to 18 months in advance. When customer demand or research needs exceed that capacity, it procures short-term compute, which currently costs significantly more. The filing expects its use of short-term compute to keep rising in the near term. That is a different story from a bubble bear's, and it is not a comfortable one either. My inference, not the filing's wording: a premium for short-term capacity is what a company pays when the capacity it booked falls short of what it needs.

Here is the arithmetic I did on those figures. It is my own, not the filing's. The $69.8 billion due in the next 12 months is about 8 percent of the $895.4 billion total. The $447.5 billion expected before the end of 2030 is about half. So roughly half of the commitment falls after 2030, and that is the part a later filing is most likely to change. On the quarter, the $567 million loss is about 3 percent of the $17.0 billion revenue. A 15 percent adjusted margin on that revenue comes to about $2.5 billion, which matches the adjusted figure the filing gives. The gap between a GAAP loss and an adjusted profit is the set of adjustments the filing reconciles, not an arithmetic error.

The third item is the self-improvement passage, which is the part most likely to be quoted out of context. The filing says that "expectations regarding future models, rather than our current models, indicate that we may imminently meet" the definition of dramatically accelerated progress attributable to AI in its Responsible Scaling Policy. It also says that Anthropic is "evaluating the implications of meeting the AI research and development capability threshold". It is equally explicit on the other side: "We have not designated any of our current models as exceeding this threshold". Separately, the filing says "we do not anticipate being able to safely use Claude to fully autonomously design and build successive generations of frontier models without meaningful human supervision". The sentence goes on to make that conditional on understanding, if and when such a thing becomes technically possible. It says the company is considering updates to its scaling policy. Those are the company's words, and they are more hedged than a headline would be.

The fourth item is a counterparty. The filing says that between May and October 2026, Anthropic entered into and amended several agreements with SpaceX.AI LLC, formerly X.AI LLC, a subsidiary of Space Exploration Technologies Corp., to access compute powered by NVIDIA GPUs. Under those agreements, Anthropic agreed to spend "up to $120.5 billion over terms extending into" 2029. The filing puts the cost at approximately $3.1 billion in aggregate per month once all capacity has been delivered, which it expects in December 2026, with different fees during the ramp. The agreements have initial non-cancelable periods ending in January 2027 and March 2027. After those, either party may cancel on 90 days' advance written notice.

That total does not divide cleanly, and I want to say so. At $3.1 billion a month, and assuming the terms run to the end of 2029, which the filing does not state exactly, full-rate spend from December 2026 is about $115 billion, which leaves roughly $6 billion of the $120.5 billion unexplained by full-rate months. The filing's ramp-period fees may account for that gap. The filing does not give the breakdown, so I cannot tell you which it is.

The fifth item is the Broadcom structure. The filing says Anthropic has guaranteed obligations of certain subsidiaries that are party to AI infrastructure arrangements. It also describes an equipment lease with Broadcom, entered into in March 2026, to procure specialized AI accelerators. Broadcom has assigned a portion of that arrangement to a designated financial partner, and may assign more. The filing says Anthropic may issue, if needed, "up to $42.0 billion in aggregate principal amount" of convertible promissory notes to Broadcom or a designated partner to finance obligations under that lease. The filing makes the notes subject to conditions, including revenue growth rate conditions and a minimum liquidity requirement, and says they are to be issued "solely to finance quarterly rent payments" under the lease. It puts the rent over that five-year equipment lease at about $125.2 billion, excluding interest. The financing is now in the filing. What it does to the company's balance sheet is a separate question, and I have not finished the accounting.

Now the things I could not confirm. One press account put Anthropic's third-quarter compute bill at $10 billion. I could not find that figure in the filing, so I am not repeating it. The filing's quarterly compute figure is the $5.0 billion of short-term compute, which is a narrower measure. I also cannot give you a total compute spend for the quarter, because the filing does not state one.

Here is what I am watching, and what would change my read.

The first thing is the filing's own next version. This is an amendment, so there may be more. The test is whether later versions keep the $895.4 billion total, and whether the $5.0 billion short-term compute cost comes down in the next quarter. If the cost falls, the company's explanation of the loss holds. If it does not, the explanation was a timing story that did not end.

The second thing is the SpaceXAI schedule. The filing expects full capacity in December 2026, and the initial non-cancelable periods end in January and March 2027. Those are dates I can check. If capacity arrives late, the cost structure gets worse before it gets better. If either party uses the 90-day cancellation right, that is the first sign a counterparty has decided it does not want the contract. I will score both against the next filing, not against a press summary.

The third is the self-improvement language. The filing ties its statement that the company "may imminently meet" the threshold to future models rather than current ones, and says it has not designated any current model as exceeding it. That is a claim with a test built in. A later filing or report that says a model has been designated would be checkable against the policy. I am not forecasting whether that happens. The filing has set up the question, and the wording will matter when it is answered.

The fourth is dilution. The filing says that delivering shares on conversion of the convertible notes "could result in dilution to our existing stockholders." If the Broadcom notes are issued, the question for a public holder becomes how many shares they add, and at what price. I will track that from the filing, not from the press.

On price and valuation, I have not found a price range in the filing, so I am not forecasting one today. I will not enter a new tracked call on this episode.

The lesson for an operator is about where a commitment shows up first. For Anthropic, the commitment shows up in a filing as a number and a schedule. It shows up in a financing structure as a guarantee and a convertible note. It shows up in a cost base as a short-term price premium. A small firm that buys from a lab, or depends on one for its own software, usually meets the commitment at the last of those stages. The filing lets you see it at the first.

The practical question for a buyer is not the headline total. It is the terms underneath: whether your price can change if the vendor pays more for its own capacity, what notice the vendor owes you before it changes terms, and who carries the cost if a hardware supplier of the vendor's is late. In my reading, a vendor's supply problems can reach its customers' prices, and the filing is a useful reminder of that.

Take the three questions in turn, as a checklist you could run this week against any AI vendor you use. First, price change: does the contract let the vendor raise prices on notice, and is that notice measured in days or in quarters? Second, supply failure: if the vendor's capacity arrives late, do your service levels change, and do you get a credit? Third, exit: can you leave on short notice, and what do you lose if you do? The filing gives 90-day cancellation rights under the SpaceXAI arrangements, after their initial non-cancelable periods. Its Amazon commitment, by contrast, is non-cancelable except for Amazon's uncured material breach. Notice terms like the 90-day one are a term a buyer can ask for, too.
