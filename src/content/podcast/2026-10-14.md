Start with Anthropic. On October twelfth the company published a research post on self-replicating AI. Its central sentence reads: "Our experiments suggest that small AI models may be months away from being capable enough to self-replicate." The post has two experiments. In a demo, it runs a Gemma model split across fifteen inexpensive off-brand Android TV boxes, about fifty-five dollars each. That demo shows inference across cheap hardware, not replication. The replication test ran on eight cloud nodes running Android emulators, with a model acting as the worm that spreads a payload model from node to node. The post reports the worm reached all eight nodes in four of five trials, and seven of eight in the fifth. It says a replicator like this "would be challenging to shut down, since there's no centralized point to shut off."

Now the qualifier. The post says its simplified evaluation keeps the cyber-exploit step trivial, and it calls that choice an upper bound on real-world success. So the trial results are measured inside a simplified setup, and they are a ceiling for the real world, not a rate. The timeline is also the lab's own estimate: the post says that if small models improve at their historical rate, they will match the end-to-end capability it describes by the end of this year. My read is that this is a careful internal test with a published method, and I found no outside repeat of it. Treat the timeline as Anthropic's forecast, not as something that has happened.

Put the day's three main stories side by side. A lab reports a simulated worm that spread a model across emulator nodes, and separately shows a model running across cheap TV boxes. The supply-chain version, flagged as my analogy. The replication post says the danger is that there is no centralized point to shut off. Think of a supplier network with no single contact and no one who owns the stop-shipment call. The trouble is not any one part. It is that nobody can halt the flow quickly, and nobody can say who released what. A distributed agent that can copy itself is that problem with a software face.

What this means for you, as suggestions and not practices I have seen work. First, for any AI tool that can act on orders, inventory, or supplier communications, write down who can pause it, how fast, and where its action log lives. Do that before you need it. Second, if a vendor tells you a model is available, ask which version is the one behind the API and which version is behind a subscription, because the two can differ in what they can do and in what you can audit. Third, the criminal case is a reminder that an office is a site with access points. If your team discusses supplier pricing or production plans in rooms that a visitor or contractor can reach, a hidden device gets past every software control you have. Check the access list before you check the software.
