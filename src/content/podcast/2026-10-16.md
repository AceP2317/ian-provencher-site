The first primary is Anthropic's post of October fifteenth, "An update on independent evaluators." The company says it has "started to give employee-like access to a team from METR, a nonprofit that evaluates frontier AI models." Its own description is six METR employees with company hardware, office space, and internal tools "roughly on par with what our own risk assessment teams have access to." The qualifiers sit in the same post. "Anthropic can't veto publication, but we can require them to redact information if it falls into narrow, predefined categories." Those categories are named earlier in the post: data about customers, attorney-client privileged information, and information about model architectures. The post adds that METR "is free to characterize our redactions publicly, and say whether it believes the redactions were justified," without revealing the redacted information. And: "Our engagement with METR is limited to a small team operating under enforceable agreements." The post also says METR will publish the results of its risk assessments. It also says Anthropic signed the White House Accord on Super Intelligence, which it describes as a pledge to work with independent external evaluators. The post gives no date for the signing.

So the independence of this check depends on two things I cannot test from here: what the redactions remove in practice, and whether METR's published results will show what it was able to see. The announcement is the company's description. The METR publication is the thing to read when it appears.

Today's four primaries share a question I did not plan to ask: who can change the report after the check is done? An outside evaluator with staff-level access sounds like independence. The post itself says the company can require redactions in defined categories, and that the evaluator's access is limited to a small team under enforceable agreements. Those are the clauses that decide what the outside reader gets.

Here is the supply-chain version, flagged as my analogy. A second-party audit where the supplier can require redactions in defined categories before the report goes out is not the same as a third-party audit with the report going to you. The auditor's access tells you how much the auditor saw. The redaction right tells you how much of what they saw you will read. A planner who treats both as one assurance has bought a checkmark, not a check.

What this means for you, as suggestions and not practices I have seen work. First, when a vendor says it has an independent check, ask who controls publication and what the redaction categories are, the way you would ask who holds the release authority on a part. Second, put vendor model retirement dates into your cost and tooling calendar from the vendor's own table, not from the announcement. A ninety-day window is short when the replacement changes behaviour. Third, when a source is a site with sub-pages, read the page that holds the section you need. Yesterday's correction came from exactly that gap.
