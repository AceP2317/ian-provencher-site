The story I would put first today is about how fast the physical base of AI is still growing. The answer is still fast, but it is no longer at the 2023 pace, and the difference matters more to an operator than the headline suggests.

Epoch AI published a data insight on 29 October headlined "New global AI computing capacity is doubling every 9 months". The authors are Isabel Juniewicz and Josh You, and the numbers use median estimates as of 19 October. The series is quarterly sales of AI chips from six designers: Nvidia, Google, AMD, Amazon, Huawei and Cambricon. Each chip is converted into H100-equivalents, so one unit is the computing capacity of one Nvidia H100. I went through the page and its data table in full. I did not download the CSV, so every figure below comes from the table printed on the page.

The trend is the thing to remember. The page says: "Quarterly additions of computing capacity have grown about 2.5× per year since 2024." It sets that against the earlier surge, which was "roughly 6× in 2023", when the first ChatGPT and GPT-4 wave pulled in a large amount of training and inference capacity, led by Nvidia's H100. The fitted trend is 2.5 times a year, with a 90 percent interval of 2.3 to 2.7 times, which the authors translate into a doubling time of 9.1 months. The fit rests on ten quarters. As the page puts it: "Quarterly compute capacity additions rose from 0.8 million H100e in Q1 2024 to 6.3 million in Q2 2026."

The table shows where the recent number came from, and the mix has moved. In Q1 2024, Nvidia's line was about 573,000 H100e out of a total of about 828,000, roughly 69 percent. In Q2 2026, Nvidia was about 3.95 million out of 6.33 million, roughly 62 percent. Nvidia's line grew about sevenfold over that period. Google's line grew about thirteenfold, from about 122,000 to about 1.61 million. The page's text says both Nvidia GPUs and Google TPUs surged during 2023, so the Google line is the one to watch if you want to know whether the buyers are switching silicon or just buying more. The Q2 2026 total was up about 27 percent on Q1 2026, from 4.98 million to 6.33 million. Google's line alone rose about 74 percent in that single quarter.

One row in the table reads as a data choice rather than a measurement. Huawei's line shows exactly 131,964 H100e for four quarters running, from Q1 2025 through Q4 2025, before moving to 202,416 in Q1 2026. A figure that does not change for a year looks like an estimate carried forward. The page does not say that, so I am reading it as my own inference, and it is small next to the Nvidia and Google lines.

Now the slowdown, which is where the page hedges. Year-over-year growth in quarterly additions fell "from 2.8× in Q2 2025 to 2.2× in Q2 2026". The authors then write: "Some evidence suggests further deceleration in growth over this period, but it is not statistically significant and may also reflect a lull between generations." They fit a declining quarterly growth rate, from about 32 percent to 19 percent per quarter, and report a p-value of 0.26. The page is also candid that the question is open: "it is unclear whether they have slowed since the beginning of 2024 or whether the growth rate has been steady." So the direction points down, the evidence for the drop is weak, and the authors offer a possible cause, a lull between generations, without ruling out a real slowdown. A "lull between generations" is a specific claim about timing: the page ties the noise to rollouts such as Nvidia's Blackwell in late 2024 and early 2025. That is checkable against the next two quarters.

The page also gives a cumulative-capacity figure. Cumulative capacity "grew 2.6× between Q2 2025 and Q2 2026, close to the 2.5× trend in quarterly additions and down from 3.5× the year before." The authors chose the quarterly series on purpose. Cumulative growth can diverge from the trend after a sales surge, as it did after 2023, and the cumulative stock was small relative to recent sales in 2024 and 2025. They say a fit to cumulative capacity "would overstate it." I would quote both numbers together if I were asked to summarise the build-out in one sentence: the stock is still growing about 2.6 times a year, and the flow of new chips is growing about 2.5 times a year.

Two caveats matter more to an operator than the trend. The first is timing. The page says: "Our data tracks chips when they are sold, not when they come online." It adds: "Chip sellers like Nvidia typically recognize revenue for AI chips when they deliver them to a server assembly partner, so those chips can take several more months to become operational in a data center." So a quarter's additions measure what has left the vendor's hands. The compute a customer can actually run arrives later, and by the page's own account the lag can be several months. The second caveat is coverage. The page omits "Intel, Meta, Microsoft, Cerebras, and Groq", and says those volumes are minor and "would not meaningfully change this trend." It also says its confidence intervals "reflect only how well the trend fits our median sales estimate, not uncertainty in the underlying sales estimates." The sales estimates are built from company financial disclosures, analyst estimates and media reports. The intervals therefore leave out the uncertainty in the sales estimates themselves.

On Nvidia's newest chips, the page says: "While Nvidia has recently started shipping its next-generation Rubin chips, it had not yet recognized any Rubin sales as of its latest fiscal quarter ending in July." That is Epoch's statement. I did not open Nvidia's own filing, so I am attributing it to Epoch and not confirming it. The practical point is that the July quarter is the one before any volume Rubin revenue would appear, which makes the next Nvidia quarter the first real test. That test is on the scorecard below.

Here is the supply-chain version of the chip story. In a plant, a machine is not capacity when the vendor ships it. It becomes capacity when it is installed, commissioned and qualified on the parts you need. The ERP view is the same. A goods receipt is not available capacity, and a planned order is not a work centre that can run. Epoch's series counts the machines leaving the vendor's dock, and the page says so. When a client asks how much AI capacity is being added, that is the first question to answer, because the answer changes depending on which stage they mean.

The page's "lull between generations" is, in plant terms, a changeover dip. A line that switches tooling loses output for a few weeks, and a single quarter's year-over-year number can read low for reasons other than demand. The page itself ties the quarter-to-quarter noise to rollouts such as Blackwell in late 2024 and early 2025, which is the same effect. My own advice to anyone reading the series is to ignore one quarter and watch the trend over three or four. That is my judgment, not the authors' advice, and their confidence intervals cover only the fit.

The mix shift is worth noticing too. If Google's line keeps growing faster than Nvidia's, the buyers are moving volume between suppliers, and a plan built on one vendor's ship dates becomes a plan built on two. For a manufacturing client who schedules around a single component vendor, the lesson is the one I would give any sourcing team: a supplier's share of your demand is a number you should track separately from total demand.

For the S-1, the method I would apply is the one I would use on a customer's annual report. The announcement tells you a name and an exchange. The disclosures tell you who the customer depends on, what it has committed to buy, and what could stop it. For a supplier, those sections are the purchase commitments, the concentration note and the risk factors. I will read those when the filing is public, and I will tell you which of the three I could not reach if any of them are still out of my reach.

What I would take from today is simple. Capacity added to the AI build-out is still growing at about 2.5 times a year on a shipments basis, and the slowdown the page describes is not yet proven. Anthropic has named its exchange symbol, and its announcement gives no offering size or price. The first number to read in the next quarter is the one that tells you whether the shipments are running, not only leaving.
