Today's main story is a pricing page, not a launch. Google's Gemini API pricing page carries a last-updated stamp of October twenty-second, and it sets out what a developer pays for Argon after a lower rate ends. The page calls Argon "Our flagship reasoning model", built for complex software engineering, deep mathematical and scientific problem solving, and autonomous multi-step agents.

Argon itself is not new this week. Google's launch post, dated October thirteenth, says "Starting today, developers can build with Gemini 4 Argon". What changed on the twenty-second is the price table, and that is the part a budget has to read.

Through November thirtieth, Argon's standard input costs two dollars per million tokens and its output costs ten dollars per million. The page then lists the higher rate as "starting December 1, 2026": four dollars input and twenty dollars output. That is a doubling of both, about seven weeks after launch. The other tiers move the same way. Batch and Flex input go from one dollar to two, and their output from five dollars to ten. Context caching goes from ten cents to twenty cents per million tokens. Every row I checked doubles on the same day.

The launch comparison is the one that makes the model look cheap. Google's own pricing page lists its predecessor, Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview, at two dollars input and twelve dollars output for prompts the page sizes at 200k tokens, and calls it the 3rd generation Pro model. Argon's standard rows show no size split, so I compare them as listed. At launch Argon's input matched the predecessor and its output was lower. From December first, both prices sit above it. A budget that carries the launch comparison forward is carrying the cheap month, not the normal one.

The cheaper tier moves too. The page calls Gemini 3.8 Flash "Our most intelligent Flash model", and its prices rise "starting January 1, 2027." Input goes from seventy-five cents to one dollar and fifty cents per million tokens. Output goes from three dollars and seventy-five cents to seven dollars and fifty cents. That is next year's problem, but it is the next budget cycle, not a footnote.

I'm not making a call about what the OpenAI tiers will do to anyone's bill. The table tells me the prices. It doesn't tell me the volume, and I'd be guessing.

A lower rate that expires works like a promotional price on a purchased part. The first lot is at the promotional rate, and the standard price takes over on a date written in the contract. A planner who builds the quarter's budget on the first lot will be surprised when the next lot arrives. The fix is the same as for parts: put the expiry date next to every AI line in the budget, and cost the line at the rate that applies after the date, not the rate that applies today.

For Argon, that means a December line should carry four dollars input and twenty dollars output for standard calls, and the matching doubled rates for batch and caching. If a workload was planned around the November rate, its cost roughly doubles in December with no change in what the workload does. That is a budget failure, not a technology failure, and it is cheap to prevent once you see it coming.

The tier choice is a purchasing decision with a wide spread on one model. For GPT-6.1 Sol, batch input is one dollar, Fast is four, and Ultrafast is twelve, so the top tier costs twelve times the bottom on input. Batch at half the standard rate fits work that does not need an answer in the session, such as an overnight exception triage run after a planning cycle. Fast at double fits the cases where the answer is needed before someone moves on. Choose the tier per job, not per vendor, because the same model can sit in both categories depending on the job.

The cheap tier fits high-volume sorting. Luna's ten cents of input and fifty cents of output per million tokens make it the candidate for classifying large batches of messages, lines, or records. Before you pay for a flagship on that job, test the cheap model on a sample you have already checked by hand. If it matches the sample at the rate you need, you have found the cheapest qualified source for the job. If it doesn't, you have learned that cheaply, and you can still move up to the flagship with a clear reason.

Mistral's weights are a different kind of decision. If they ship at the end of October, the cost question changes shape. You stop paying per token to a vendor and start paying to host the model, and the data stays on your own systems. Whether that is worth it depends on who runs the hardware and who answers when it fails. A preview release is not the moment to decide that. I'd wait for the weights to appear and for the license to be read, then price the hosting honestly against the per-token figure.

The practical step for this week is small. Pull up every AI line in your budget and write its expiry date beside it, if it has one. Re-cost each line at the rate that applies after that date. For a single team, that takes about ten minutes, and it is the cheapest way to find out whether a December surprise is already in the plan.
